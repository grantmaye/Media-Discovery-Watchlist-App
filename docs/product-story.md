# Frame: remembering why a film caught your eye

A watchlist is often a collection of titles with no memory attached. A person saves something, forgets why, and returns to a longer list. Frame explores a smaller, more deliberate workflow: discover a film, make a plan to watch it, track that intention, and leave a personal note after finishing.

This is a hypothetical product story grounded in the implementation. It is not the author's claimed origin story and does not imply customers, revenue, a film-distribution business, licensed posters, or production usage. All twenty films, directors, descriptions, and release details in [catalog.ts](../src/lib/catalog.ts) are fictional. The graphic covers are generated by [CSS](../src/app/globals.css).

## A hypothetical film-club member

Imagine a film-club member preparing for a weekend discussion. They have several recommendations scattered across messages and notes. One sounded visually interesting, another came from a friend, and a third was started but never finished. A flat list does not distinguish those states or preserve the reason for keeping a title.

In Frame, the person searches the small catalog, narrows by genre, and opens a title to inspect its description. They save it as Up next, later mark it Watching, then Finished. Only finished films receive ratings. A short note records what stayed with them. When they return, that note is still there because watchlist entries persist on the server.

This narrative is an illustration of intended use; the actual software cannot fetch those friends' recommendations, play films, or tell anyone where they are streaming. It demonstrates the journal workflow with reproducible sample content.

## What the software contributes

[discovery.tsx](../src/components/discovery.tsx) keeps the experience focused on a modest collection rather than an endless feed. Large editorial headings, forest-green accents, warm paper, and geometric covers distinguish Frame from an operations console or a studio calendar.

The backend adds guarantees that are easy to overlook in a small personal tool. One workspace cannot create duplicate entries for the same title. A stale tab cannot silently overwrite a newer note. Removal preserves a versioned tombstone so re-adding a title does not reset its identity and admit an old edit. Pagination cursors belong to their filter set, and a delayed page cannot overwrite a newer search. See [service.ts](../src/lib/service.ts) and the [browser regression](../tests/e2e/workflow.spec.ts).

| Hypothetical before                        | Implemented after                           |
| ------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------- |
| Titles scattered across messages           | A searchable, bounded discovery catalog     |
| One undifferentiated saved list            | Up next, Watching, and Finished states      |
| Forgetting why a film mattered             | Personal notes beside finished-film ratings |
| Accidental duplicate saves                 | One workspace/title record                  |
| An old tab overwrites a newer thought      | Expected-version conflict                   |
| Clearing a title loses concurrency history | Hidden tombstone with a continuing version  |

These are intended workflow benefits, not measured improvements in engagement or viewing satisfaction.

## Who can use this example

A developer learning full-stack design can trace a complete application without obtaining a commercial movie API key. A reviewer can ask about stable pagination, optimistic concurrency, and client response races using a concrete interface. A product designer can evaluate how a journal-like presentation changes the meaning of “save for later.” A real film enthusiast can explore the demo, but cannot use its fictional catalog as factual movie information.

The limitations are deliberate and visible: no accounts, no social reviews, no shared lists, no recommendations, no streaming availability, and no playback. A browser cookie identifies a demo workspace; it is not a secure account. Removal is not a privacy erasure operation. The [technical manual](technical-manual.md) explains these distinctions and provides extension exercises.

## A 60–90 second demo

**0–15 seconds:** “Frame is a small film-journal portfolio demo. Every film and cover here is original fictional content, so no external movie API key is needed.” Point to the editorial hero and catalog.

**15–35 seconds:** Search “Static Bloom,” then open it. “Search and genre filters return a bounded page. Cursors are tied to the selected filters, so a continuation cannot silently mix another result set.” Save the film as Up next.

**35–55 seconds:** Open Your watchlist, mark the film Finished, select a rating, and add a short note. “This score is my own, not a claimed critic or community rating.” Save and reload to demonstrate persistence.

**55–75 seconds:** Remove and re-add the film. “Removal hides it from the list, but the backend preserves a version so an old tab cannot overwrite the new entry.” Use the service test as evidence if time allows.

**75–90 seconds:** “The interesting parts are the journal state rules, versioned tombstones, stable cursors, and handling out-of-order browser requests. This does not stream real films or authenticate users. The manual traces each of those boundaries.”

The story is strongest when the demo ends with a real saved note and one clearly explained concurrency rule, rather than an invented claim of recommendation intelligence.
